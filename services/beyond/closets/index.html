Custom Closets — Walk-Ins, Reach-Ins & Wardrobe Walls

A closet should feel like part of the house, not like furniture pushed against a wall.

Hanging space, drawers, shoe shelves and everything scribed to your walls — then trimmed with base and crown and sprayed to a factory finish, so the seams disappear and it reads as built-in.

One Wire Shelf, One Rod, and Everything Else on the Floor.

That’s the standard closet in most Puget Sound houses. A single shelf at a height nobody can reach, a rod sagging in the middle, shoes in a pile below it, and four feet of completely empty air between the top shelf and the ceiling. You’re paying for that cubic footage and using about half of it.

The frustrating part is that the fix isn’t structural. Nothing needs to move. The room is already the right size — it’s just never been fitted out, and a closet you order online arrives in standard widths that don’t match your walls, then sits there looking like a box someone left behind.

Let’s make the closet you already have hold twice as much.

What Do You Actually Get With a Custom Closet?

Storage matched to what you own, not to a standard rod height. We start by asking what actually has to go in there, then lay the closet out around the answer:

  • Double-hung and full-height hanging — short hanging stacked two deep where it works, long hanging kept only where you need it
  • Drawer banks — so folded clothes stop competing with a dresser in the bedroom
  • Shoe shelves — angled or flat, sized to the shoes rather than to whatever the shelf pin holes allow
  • Adjustable shelving — because what you store changes, and the closet should follow
  • Closed storage up top — the dead space above the old shelf, finally doing something
  • Lighting — a fixture that shows you the actual colour of what you’re holding

How Do You Make a Closet Look Built-In Instead of Like Furniture?

Trim carpentry and paint. That’s the whole trick, and it’s where most closet installs stop short. Cabinet boxes set against a wall always give themselves away in the same four places: the seams between units, the gap over the top, the toe kick, and the point where the run meets the existing baseboard.

So we close all four. Thin strips of moulding cover the vertical seams between units. Filler strips bridge the cabinets to the ceiling. Base moulding wraps the run and ties into the room’s existing trim, and crown finishes the top. Then everything gets masked, spackled, and caulked at every joint, and the new trim is sprayed — not brushed — in paint colour-matched to the cabinet fronts.

Done properly, you can’t tell where the cabinetry ends and the carpentry starts. That is the entire difference between a closet that looks bought and one that looks built.

Anyone can stand cabinets against a wall. What makes it a built-in is the day and a half of trim, caulk and paint that happens after.

What If the Closet Isn’t a Simple Rectangle?

That’s usually where the storage is hiding. Angled walls under a roofline, a chimney chase bumping into the corner, a sloped ceiling, a door that swings the wrong way — these are the reasons an off-the-shelf closet kit stops two feet short and leaves a triangle of dead space.

On the Newcastle closet one wall ran at 45 degrees. Stopping the cabinets square would have left an awkward wedge doing nothing, so we modified the end unit and custom-cut the shelves to follow the angle right into the corner. It took longer. It also gained a full bay of shelving and, more than any other detail, it’s what sells the built-in look.

Odd geometry is the argument for building it in place rather than against it — the same reason our custom built-ins get scribed to the wall instead of set against it.

Do You Do Reach-Ins and Bedroom Wardrobe Walls Too?

Yes — and in older Seattle houses that’s often the only option. Plenty of homes here were built with a shallow reach-in closet or, in the oldest ones, no bedroom closet at all. When there’s no room to walk into, the wardrobe goes onto the wall instead.

A wardrobe wall is built the same way as a walk-in: full-height cabinetry, scribed to the wall, trimmed into the existing base and crown, and finished to match the room. Behind the doors the fit-out is identical. The wardrobe in this photo is shown open — double-hung rods on the left, a bank of drawers and a niche in the centre, pull-out racks on the right for shoes and folded clothes. Closed, it reads as a flush wall.

The style follows the house rather than a catalogue. In one Snoqualmie home we matched the run to the existing craftsman moulding and wainscoting, and worked a built-in desk nook into the middle of a child’s wardrobe — a base cabinet cut down so the desktop landed at a comfortable 30 inches. What Does a Custom Closet Cost?

Less than most people assume, because nothing structural moves. No walls come down, no plumbing gets rerouted, and in most cases no permit is needed. A closet is one of the few remodels where almost all the money goes into what you can see.

What moves the number is the cabinetry itself and how much of it there is: drawer banks cost more than shelves, inset doors cost more than slab, and a run of full-height units costs more than a rod with shelving above it. Full custom cabinetry buys you any dimension and any wood species. A hybrid route — modular cabinet boxes as the carcass, then custom panels, fillers, trim and colour-matched paint over the top — gets the same built-in result for meaningfully less, and it’s how a good number of these get done.
