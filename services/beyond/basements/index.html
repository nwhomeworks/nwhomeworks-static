The Square Footage You Already Own

A basement is the cheapest square footage you will ever add to a house. It already has a roof, a foundation, and walls. Finishing it costs a fraction of building out, and in Seattle and on the Eastside it can turn into a rental, a place for family, or simply the part of the house everyone actually uses.

The kitchen at the top of this page is a Mercer Island basement we converted into a full apartment. Gas range, dishwasher, laundry in the same run of cabinets, a gas fireplace, and a window pulling real daylight into a floor that used to have none. That is the difference between a finished basement and a basement someone can live in.

ADUs and Mother-in-Law Suites

Most basement conversions come down to one question: does it need a kitchen? Once it does, you are into plumbing, venting, dedicated circuits, and permits, and the project stops being a finish job. That is exactly the work we do in-house, which is why we take it on.

This NE Seattle basement got a compact kitchen built to do everything a full one does: induction cooktop, sink, microwave, butcher block counters, and enough cabinet storage that nothing lives on the countertop. Running the supply lines, drain, and circuits to a below-grade room is the hard part, and it is the same problem-solving we used to put a full bathroom into an attic.

Media Rooms, Play Rooms & Built-In Storage

Not every basement needs to become an apartment. This one had a large, awkward wall that had quietly turned into the family catch-all. Our designer worked with the homeowners to fill it with a two-tiered media center: cabinets to the ceiling, a recessed opening sized for the TV, and closed storage running the full length of the wall.

Every media cable tucks into the chase behind the cabinets and the components sit behind doors, so the wall reads as millwork rather than electronics. Read how we built it, or see more of what we do with built-ins and storage.

What Makes a Basement Harder Than It Looks

Below-grade work has its own rules. Ceiling height is fixed, so ductwork and plumbing have to be routed rather than relocated. Moisture has to be handled before a single piece of drywall goes up. Bedrooms need egress. Add a kitchen or a bath and you are coordinating plumbing, electrical, and inspections in a space with no margin.
