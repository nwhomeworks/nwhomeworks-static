Is Your Basement Still Storage, Spiders, and a Furnace?

Bare concrete. A single bulb on a pull chain. Boxes you haven’t opened since you moved in. Meanwhile you’re paying a Seattle mortgage on square footage nobody in the family will voluntarily walk into. It doesn’t have to stay that way.

A basement is the least expensive space you will ever add to a house, because the expensive parts — foundation, walls, roof — are already built and paid for. The catch is that below-grade work has its own rules, and most of what makes it hard stays invisible until somebody opens a wall.

Let’s turn the floor you avoid into the one everyone uses.

What’s Included in a Basement Conversion?

Everything from framing to final walkthrough, under one roof. We’re a design-build general contractor with our trades in-house, which matters more downstairs than almost anywhere else in a house.

  • Framing & layout — walls, soffits to hide ductwork, and egress windows or doors wherever bedrooms are planned
  • Moisture management — handled before insulation and drywall, not discovered after
  • Plumbing & electrical — licensed in-house trades, dedicated circuits, permits handled
  • Kitchens & kitchenettes — full appliance runs, or a compact prep kitchen for an ADU
  • Bathrooms — including below-grade drainage and proper shower waterproofing
  • Built-ins & finish carpentry — media walls, storage, trim, and doors

One project manager, one schedule, one crew. You don’t chase subcontractors and we don’t pass blame.

Can You Put a Full Kitchen in a Basement?

Yes — and it’s the decision that turns a finished basement into an actual dwelling. Once a kitchen is in the plan you’re into supply lines, drainage, venting, dedicated circuits, and permits, and the project stops being a finish job. That’s exactly the work we do in-house, which is why we take it on.

The Mercer Island basement at the top of this page became a full apartment: gas range, dishwasher, laundry in the same run of cabinets, a gas fireplace, and a window pulling real daylight into a floor that had none. The NE Seattle basement in this photo went the compact route, with an induction cooktop, sink, microwave, butcher block counters, and enough storage that nothing has to live on the countertop.

Getting plumbing and power into a room that was never designed for either is the same problem-solving we used to put an award-winning bathroom into an attic.

Every house has a floor nobody uses. Finishing it is the only remodel that gives you a whole new room without moving a single exterior wall.

What Makes a Basement Harder Than It Looks?

Ceiling height is the constraint everything else bends around. Upstairs you relocate a duct. Downstairs you route around it, box it into a soffit, or give up headroom you can’t spare. The same goes for beams, waste lines, and the main panel.

Moisture comes next, and it has to be solved before insulation and drywall rather than found afterward. Then there’s egress: any room you intend to call a bedroom needs a code-compliant way out, which often means cutting a new window well into the foundation.

What Drives the Cost of a Basement Remodel?

Scope moves the number far more than square footage does. A basement that already has height, dry walls, and a bathroom rough-in is a finish job. One that needs a new egress window cut into the foundation, a kitchen plumbed from scratch, and ductwork rerouted is a different project at exactly the same size.

The biggest single jump is adding a kitchen or a bathroom, because that’s where permits, licensed trades, and inspections enter the picture. Egress windows, moisture remediation, and low headroom are the three that tend to surprise people after that.

We give fixed-price proposals after a free in-home consultation, laid out line by line, so you know which of those apply to your basement before anything starts.
